Kālavidyā: The Science of Time in Sanātana Dharma
Part 2: Pañcāṅgam

English · Sanskrit · Article · Indic · Vedic Science · 06.04.2026

The Five Limbs of Time

तिथिवारं च नक्षत्रं योगः करणमेव च । पञ्चाङ्गमिति विख्यातं कालज्ञानस्य साधनम् ॥ ~ पञ्चाङ्गस्य परिभाषा | Pañcāṅgasya Paribhāṣā

The above verse delineates the five fundamental components of the Hindu almanac: the lunar day (tithiḥ), the solar day of the week (vāraḥ), the stellar mansion (nakṣatram), the luni-solar alignment (yogaḥ), and the half-lunar day (karaṇam). Together, these five (pañca) limbs (aṅgam) constitute the pañcāṅgam, the essential instrument for the comprehension of time.

Operating on the foundation of the mūrta kāla framework established in Part 1, the pañcāṅgam plays a vital role in everyday affairs within Sanātana Dharma. It determines auspicious and inauspicious timings for critical tasks, rituals, and festivals. This daily consultation is deeply rooted in the philosophy that terrestrial events are governed by an intricate intersection of human effort and cosmic synchronisation.

1. Vāraḥ (वारः)

Ancient Indians and Babylonians observed seven primary heavenly bodies—the Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, and Saturn—and named the days of the week sequentially after them. A vāraḥ represents the solar day, measuring the time taken for the Earth to rotate once upon its axis, observed from sunrise to the subsequent sunrise. Seven of these consecutive vāra constitute a single cycle, universally recognised as a week.

2. Tithiḥ (तिथिः)

While the vāraḥ tracks the solar day, the tithiḥ measures the lunar day. One tithiḥ elapses when the longitudinal angle difference between the sun and the moon increases by exactly 12°. Consequently, there are thirty tithi within one synodic lunar month (approximately 29.53 solar days), reflecting the time taken for the moon to revolve around the Earth and return to its initial angular position relative to the sun.

Because the orbital velocities of the Earth and the Moon are elliptical and non-uniform, the time required to traverse this 12° separation varies. Therefore, tithi commence at varying times of the solar day and possess fluctuating durations, typically ranging from 19 to 26 hours.

3. Nakṣatram (नक्षत्रम्)

Scriptural narratives recount that Candra (the Moon God) married the twenty-seven daughters of Prajāpati Dakṣa, favouring Rohiṇī amongst them. Astronomically, a nakṣatram is a measure of the twenty-seventh division of the ecliptic (the apparent path of the sun observed from Earth). Each division corresponds to an arc of 13° 20’ (360° / 27).

The time required for the moon to transit through one such sector defines the period of one nakṣatram, averaging approximately 24 hours. These sectors are named after prominent asterisms situated within their bounds. The division into twenty-seven perfectly mirrors the sidereal month—the moon's true orbital period relative to the fixed stars, which is approximately 27.3 days.

4. Yogaḥ (योगः)

Derived from the Sanskrit root for "union," a yogaḥ refers to the period during which the combined longitudinal motion of the sun and the moon equals exactly 13° 20’ (one twenty-seventh of a full circle). Similar to the nakṣatram, there are twenty-seven distinct yoga in a cycle. Due to the varying apparent speeds of the luminaries, the duration of each yogaḥ fluctuates significantly.

5. Karaṇam (करणम्)

A karaṇam represents exactly half of a tithiḥ, measuring a 6° longitudinal displacement between the sun and the moon. There are eleven distinct karaṇa categorised into two groups: four fixed (dhruva) and seven recurring (cara).

The four fixed karaṇa occur only once per synodic month, clustered around the darkest phase: the night before the new moon, the day and night of the new moon, and the day immediately following. The remaining seven recurring karaṇa cycle sequentially, occurring eight times each throughout the remainder of the lunar month.
